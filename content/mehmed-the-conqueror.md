      Mehmed was born in Edirne in 1432, the third son of Sultan Murad II, and for
      most of his childhood nobody expected him to rule. Then both his elder
      brothers died.

      In 1444, exhausted by war, Murad did something almost unheard of: he
      abdicated in favour of his son and retired. Mehmed was twelve.

      It went badly. A crusading army marched on the Ottoman lands within months,
      and the court did not fancy its chances behind a schoolboy. Murad was
      called back to command, won the battle of Varna, and in 1446 the viziers
      removed Mehmed and put his father back on the throne properly. Mehmed was
      sent to Manisa to grow up.

      Think about what that does to a person. He had been sultan, and the men
      around him had decided he was not up to it and taken it away.

      He got it back in 1451, when Murad died. He was nineteen. The ambassadors
      who came to size him up sent home reassuring letters: an untried young man,
      no threat.

      He had, in fact, spent five years reading siege engineering.

      Constantinople was the hardest target in the world, and everyone knew it.

      Its land walls had been finished in the 400s: a moat, then an outer wall,
      then an inner wall eleven metres high and five metres thick, studded with
      towers. For a thousand years army after army had come and gone. Only once
      had the city fallen, in 1204, and that was to crusaders who were let in
      from the sea by treachery.

      But by 1453 the empire behind the walls was a shell. The city held perhaps
      50,000 people in space built for half a million. Emperor Constantine XI
      could raise around 7,000 defenders. Mehmed brought somewhere between 50,000
      and 80,000.

      And he brought Orban.

      Orban was a cannon founder, probably Hungarian, and he had gone to
      Constantine first. The Byzantines wanted his guns and could not pay for
      them. So he went to the sultan, who paid four times what he asked.

      What Orban built was a bronze bombard over eight metres long that fired a
      stone ball weighing more than half a tonne. It needed sixty oxen to move
      and took hours to reload, so it fired perhaps seven times a day. It did not
      need to be quick. Walls built to shrug off a catapult had never been asked
      to survive that.

      Mehmed had also spent the previous year building a fortress at the narrowest
      point of the Bosphorus, cutting the city off from the Black Sea. The Turks
      called it Boğazkesen — the throat-cutter.

      The siege began on 6 April 1453. The guns hammered the walls by day; the
      defenders repaired them by night, filling breaches with earth and rubble
      and anything else to hand. It went on like that for weeks, and it was very
      nearly working.

      The city's weak side was the Golden Horn, the harbour inlet to the north.
      Its walls there were thinner — but the mouth of the harbour was closed by
      an iron chain strung across on floats, and Ottoman ships could not get in.

      So on the night of 22 April, Mehmed took them over the land instead.

      His men laid a greased track of wooden rollers across the ridge behind
      Galata and dragged some seventy ships up one side of the hill and down the
      other, into the harbour, in a single night. The defenders woke to find a
      fleet inside a harbour they had sealed. It did not win the siege by itself.
      What it did was force a garrison that was already too small to spread
      itself thinner still.

      The final assault came before dawn on 29 May. Wave after wave went at the
      walls. The Genoese commander Giovanni Giustiniani, who had held the worst
      section for seven weeks, was badly wounded and carried out — and the men
      watching him go began to break. A gate was found open. The banners went up
      on the towers.

      Constantine XI was last seen throwing off his imperial regalia and going
      into the fighting as an ordinary soldier. His body was never identified.

      Mehmed rode to the Hagia Sophia, which had been a church for nine hundred
      years, and ordered it made a mosque.

      Then he did the thing that is easier to miss and mattered more. He set
      about refilling a city he had just emptied — moving in Muslims, Christians
      and Jews from across his lands, sometimes by choice and sometimes by
      order. In 1454 he appointed a new Orthodox Patriarch and let the Church
      carry on. He began building Topkapı Palace. He collected Greek and Latin
      manuscripts, spoke several languages, and in 1480 sat for a portrait by the
      Venetian painter Gentile Bellini, which now hangs in London.

      He called himself Kayser-i Rûm — Caesar of Rome — and he was not entirely
      joking. Rome had begun in 753 BC. On 29 May 1453 the last piece of it
      stopped.

      Mehmed died in 1481, aged forty-nine. Turkish has called him Fatih ever
      since. The Conqueror.
