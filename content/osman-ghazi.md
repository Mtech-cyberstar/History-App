      In 1243 a Mongol army destroyed the Seljuk Sultanate of Rum at a place
      called Köse Dağ. The sultanate did not vanish overnight — it limped on as a
      puppet for decades — but its grip on Anatolia was finished.

      What filled the gap was not one power but dozens. Small Turkish principalities,
      each a few valleys wide, each run by a warrior chief with his own horsemen.
      Historians call them *beyliks*. There were more than twenty of them.

      Osman inherited one of the smallest, somewhere around 1280, from his father
      Ertuğrul. It sat at Söğüt in the northwest — hill country, poor, and
      pressed right up against what was left of the Byzantine Empire.

      That last detail was the whole of his luck. The other beyliks were
      surrounded by other Turks, which meant every gain came at a cousin's
      expense. Osman had an actual frontier in front of him, and behind that
      frontier an empire that had been beaten down by the Fourth Crusade and had
      never really recovered.

      Nobody at the time would have picked him. He was one small chief among
      twenty, on the edge of everything, with no port, no capital and no fleet.

      On 27 July 1302, near a place the Byzantines called Bapheus, Osman met an
      imperial army that had been sent out to deal with him. He beat it.

      This is the moment the raiding chief becomes something else. Constantinople
      had noticed him and had tried to stop him, and failed. Word travelled.
      Fighters from across Anatolia began arriving to join a man who was clearly
      winning, and a beylik that could not have fielded a thousand riders started
      to grow into something that could.

      The story everyone knows is the other one.

      Osman is said to have stayed the night at the house of a holy man named
      Sheikh Edebali, and to have dreamed that a moon rose from Edebali's chest
      and sank into his own; that from his body grew a tree whose shade covered
      the world, with four mountain ranges at its roots and four rivers running
      from them. Edebali read the dream as a promise of empire, and gave Osman
      his daughter in marriage.

      Treat that story carefully. It does not appear in any surviving text from
      Osman's lifetime. It turns up around 150 years later, in histories written
      when the Ottomans already ruled an empire and wanted an origin worthy of
      it. That does not make it a lie, exactly — it makes it a founding myth,
      which is a different thing and tells you about the people telling it.

      What is solid is this. Osman died around 1323 or 1324, still without a
      proper capital. His son Orhan took the walled city of Bursa in 1326 and
      made it one.

      The state they built lasted until 1922. It is named after him: *Osmanlı*,
      the house of Osman. In English, the Ottomans.
