    body: |
      We know less about Yunus Emre than we would like. He was born somewhere in
      central Anatolia around 1238, and he died around 1320. Beyond that, almost
      everything is argument. At least six towns claim his grave, and each one is
      quite sure.

      What survives is the story people told about him, and the story is this.

      Yunus came to the lodge of a teacher called Taptuk Emre and asked to be
      taken in. He was given the plainest job there was: fetching firewood from
      the mountain. He did it for forty years.

      In all that time, the legend says, he never once brought back a crooked
      branch. When somebody finally asked him why, he said that nothing bent or
      twisted should cross the threshold of that house.

      It is almost certainly not true. It is the kind of story that grows around
      a person after they die, polished by retelling. But it tells you what
      people believed he was like, and that is its own kind of historical fact.
      They thought of him as someone who did small work carefully for a very long
      time before he said anything at all.

      Here is the part that actually mattered.

      In the Anatolia of the 1200s, a serious person wrote in Persian, or in
      Arabic. Those were the languages of poetry, of law, of learning. Turkish was
      what you spoke to your neighbour. Writing it down was a bit like writing a
      philosophy book in slang.

      Yunus Emre wrote in Turkish. Not the elaborate court Turkish that came
      later — the ordinary spoken kind, in simple syllable-counted lines that fit
      the rhythm of folk songs. Anyone could follow them. More to the point,
      anyone could *remember* them, which is why they survived at all: they were
      sung across Anatolia for centuries before most of them were ever written
      down.

      The most quoted line attributed to him is only eight words in Turkish:

      > **We love the created, for the sake of the Creator.**

      Another, even shorter: *let us love, and let us be loved.*

      He was a Sufi, part of a tradition inside Islam concerned less with rules
      than with the direct experience of God. But the reason he is on Turkish
      banknotes and in Turkish schoolbooks is not only religion. It is that he
      proved the language people actually spoke could carry serious thought.
      Every Turkish writer since has been working in a door he pushed open.

      In 1991, UNESCO named the whole year after him.
